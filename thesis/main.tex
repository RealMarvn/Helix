\begin{abstract}
Chess engines rely heavily on heuristic search techniques to cope with the
exponential growth of the game tree. Among these techniques, iterative deepening
combined with alpha-beta pruning has become a standard approach due to its
ability to produce strong moves under strict time constraints.

This thesis presents the design and implementation of a modular chess engine and
investigates the relationship between search time and decision quality. The
central question is where the sweet spot between invested time and move quality
lies: how much additional search time actually translates into better decisions,
and at which point further computation no longer changes the chosen move.

The engine is implemented from scratch in \texttt{C++} using a classical
mailbox-based board representation. Its baseline integrates established
techniques such as alpha-beta pruning, transposition tables, quiescence search,
heuristic move ordering, and a two-stage time management system with soft and
hard deadlines. On top of this baseline, the thesis implements and evaluates three
further search refinements: Principal Variation Search (PVS), which verifies all but the
presumed best move with inexpensive null-window searches; null-move pruning,
which discards subtrees that remain winning even after passing a turn; and the
reuse of search trees from previous searches, realized through a persistent
transposition table that allows the engine to resume earlier computations.

The evaluation combines a 400-game match against a strength-limited reference
engine, which the engine wins by $205 \pm 38$ Elo, with systematic measurements
on a suite of 309 positions across time budgets from 10\,ms to 5\,s. Move quality
is scored against a strong reference engine as centipawn loss.

The results separate the two halves of the question. Both refinements deliver
depth as intended: null-move pruning gains a full ply at a one-second budget and
the persistent transposition table a further $0.25$ ply, with confidence
intervals clear of zero. For neither could a corresponding improvement in move
quality be detected. Two observations explain this. Decisions settle early, three
quarters of all positions have fixed their move by the depth the engine already
reaches. And the residual error is highly concentrated: the median centipawn loss
is $2$ while the mean is $52$, with fifteen positions accounting for nearly half
of that mean. These are predominantly sparse endgames, and they respond to
additional depth far more weakly than the rest of the suite.

Under an explicit saturation criterion, typical move quality stops improving
detectably at roughly one second per move on this suite, while average quality
keeps falling through that difficult minority. Beyond this point additional time
continues to buy depth, but better decisions only for a small class of positions,
which is consistent with the evaluation rather than the search becoming the
binding constraint.
\end{abstract}
