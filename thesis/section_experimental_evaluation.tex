\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

This section presents the experimental evaluation of the implemented chess
engine. The evaluation pursues the central question of this thesis: where lies
the sweet spot between invested search time and decision quality, and how do the
individual search refinements shift this trade-off? The strongest empirical
effect is produced by null-move pruning, which is therefore examined in detail,
while the Principal Variation Search parameter sweep yields a deliberate null
result that is reported and explained rather than hidden. All measurements are
based on a common position suite and a uniform scoring methodology, so that the
individual experiments remain directly comparable.

\subsection{Research Objectives}
\label{subsec:research_objectives}

The experimental evaluation addresses the following research questions:

The experiments group around three questions. The first concerns the relationship
between computation and decision quality, the second the effect of the individual
search refinements, and the third the origin of the errors that remain.

\paragraph{Time and decision quality.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time-to-quality and depth-versus-time:} how do reachable depth
    and move quality develop as the time budget increases, and from which budget
    onward is no further improvement detectable?
    \item \textbf{Move stability:} at which search depth does the decision settle,
    measured both by the identity of the chosen move and by its distance to a
    reference evaluation?
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Effect of the search refinements and their resources.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Null-move pruning:} how does it affect reachable depth, node
    throughput, transposition table behavior, and move quality across time
    budgets?
    \item \textbf{Search tree reuse:} how much depth does a persistent
    transposition table gain over the course of a game compared to starting each
    search from an empty table?
    \item \textbf{Principal Variation Search:} how much depth does scouting gain
    over a plain full-window search, and does that gain depend on the choice of
    its two parameters?
    \item \textbf{Transposition table size:} from which table size onward does
    the available memory stop limiting the search? This calibrates the
    configuration under which the other experiments run.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Origin of the residual error.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distribution of move quality:} how is the error spread over the
    suite, and which positions account for it?
\end{itemize}

In addition, the engine is compared against a fixed reference opponent in a match.
That experiment is a sanity check on the implementation as a whole rather than a
research question in its own right, since its result depends entirely on the
chosen opponent configuration.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{subsec:common_setup}

Unless stated otherwise, all experiments share the following configuration.

\paragraph{Hardware and Build.}
All measurements were conducted on a MacBook Pro with an Apple M3 Pro processor
and 18\,GB of RAM. The engine was compiled in release mode and executed as a
single-threaded process. Apart from the parameter that is varied in a given
experiment, all engine options were kept at their defaults throughout.

\paragraph{Position Suite.}
The experiments use a fixed suite of 309 positions, stored in the repository as
\texttt{tests/\allowbreak data/\allowbreak stockfish-bench-all.epd} and derived from the position set
that Stockfish uses for its own benchmark. Each configuration is measured with three
repetitions per position to average out scheduling noise, and reported figures
are means over positions and repetitions.

The suite was chosen because it is public, fixed and therefore reproducible, but
its composition has to be kept in mind when reading the results. It contains
between 5 and 32 pieces, with a median of 19; by piece count, $25.8\,\%$ of the
positions are opening-like ($\geq 26$ pieces), $34.0\,\%$ middlegame-like, and
$40.2\,\%$ endgames (fewer than 16 pieces). The positions are also not balanced:
the median reference evaluation is $85$\,cp, only $11\,\%$ of the positions are
approximately equal ($|\text{eval}| \leq 50$\,cp) and $16\,\%$ are already
decided ($|\text{eval}| > 300$\,cp). This is a benchmark set built to exercise a
search, not a sample of the positions an engine faces in a game, and endgames in
particular are over-represented relative to ordinary play. All results in this
chapter therefore describe the behavior of the engine \emph{on this suite};
generalizing them to tournament play would require a second, game-derived
position set, which is left to future work.

\paragraph{Time Budgets.}
Time-dependent experiments sweep a fixed set of per-move budgets of
$10, 25, 50, 100,$ $250, 500, 1000, 2000$, and $5000$\,ms. This range spans from
very short thinking times, where only shallow searches are possible, to budgets
that reach depths well into the middlegame.

\paragraph{Quality Scoring.}
Because the positions have no known ground-truth best move, move quality is
measured against a strong reference. For every position, Stockfish is run to a
fixed depth of 20, single-threaded, with its default hash size and without
tablebases, to obtain a reference best move and a reference evaluation. The move
the engine actually played is scored by applying it and analysing the resulting
position to the same depth, again from the perspective of the side that moved, so
that both values are produced under identical conditions and remain comparable.
Mate scores are first converted to a large finite value, and the resulting
centipawn loss is then clamped at $1000$\,cp so that a single decided position
cannot dominate an average. The clamp applies to \emph{every} loss above that
threshold, not only to those involving a mate. At a one-second budget it is
reached in three of the 309 positions: in two of them either the reference move
or the played move leads to a forced mate, while in the third both evaluations
are ordinary and the raw difference is $1165$\,cp. All aggregate figures in this
chapter are therefore mildly conservative, since these three losses enter the
statistics smaller than they are. Two metrics are derived from this reference:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Centipawn loss (CPL):} the difference, in centipawns and from
    the perspective of the side to move, between the reference evaluation of
    Stockfish's preferred move and the reference evaluation of the move actually
    played by the engine. Lower is better; a value of $0$ means the engine played
    a move of equal reference value.
    \item \textbf{Agreement:} the fraction of positions in which the engine's
    chosen move coincides exactly with Stockfish's reference move.
\end{itemize}
CPL is the primary quality metric, as it also credits moves that differ from the
reference move but are of comparable value; agreement is reported as a
complementary, stricter indicator. \cref{fig:cpl_pipeline} traces the procedure
for one concrete position. Note that the metric never asks whether a move is
``correct'' in any absolute sense; it only asks how much worse the position
becomes compared to the best available alternative, which is what makes small
inaccuracies and outright blunders comparable on a single scale.

Since the dependent variable of every quality statement in this chapter is
produced by this reference, its stability is a limitation of the setup. Depth 20
was chosen as a compromise between reference strength and the cost of scoring
several thousand positions, but it was not validated against a deeper reference.
A sensitivity check, rescoring the suite at a substantially higher depth and
comparing how often the reference move and the resulting CPL change materially,
would be needed to rule out that individual conclusions depend on this choice.

\paragraph{Benchmark Harness.}
All experiments are driven by the dedicated benchmarking harness in the
\texttt{bench/} directory, which records for each search, among others, the
completed depth, selective depth, node and quiescence-node counts, the number of
PVS re-searches and null-move cutoffs, transposition table statistics, and the
elapsed time. The raw per-search records are written to CSV files and aggregated
offline, which keeps measurement and analysis reproducible. Each file also stores
the commit hash of the engine it was produced with, so every table in this
chapter can be traced back to a specific state of the implementation. All
measurements reported here were produced with a single build, commit
\texttt{f3270d5} of the engine repository\footnote{\url{https://github.com/RealMarvn/Helix}},
so later changes to the engine do not affect the figures given in this chapter.

\paragraph{Separate Runs.}
The individual experiments were executed as separate runs, so the same
configuration can appear with slightly different absolute numbers in different
tables; at a one-second budget the completed depth of the default configuration
differs by a few hundredths of a ply between runs. Comparisons are therefore
always drawn within a run, between configurations measured against each other,
never across tables.

\paragraph{Uncertainty.}
The dominant source of variation in these experiments is not measurement noise
but the difference between chess positions: the same configuration can lose zero
centipawns in one position and several hundred in another. Repetitions alone
therefore say little about how reliable a reported difference is. Since almost all comparisons are paired, the same positions are measured under
both configurations, uncertainty is quantified on the paired differences instead.
For each position $i$ the three repetitions are averaged first,
$x_i = \frac{1}{3}\sum_r x_{ir}$, and the paired difference
$d_i = x_{i,A} - x_{i,B}$ is formed from these per-position means; the bootstrap
then resamples the $d_i$. Reported intervals are $95\,\%$ percentile bootstrap
confidence intervals over $20\,000$ resamples, drawn over the unit that is
actually independent: positions for the suite experiments, and complete games for
the tree-reuse experiment, whose plies are clustered within games. There, the
per-ply differences are first averaged within each game and the game means are
resampled. Seeds are fixed per reported value, so the intervals are reproducible;
the analysis script is included with the implementation. An interval that
excludes zero is reported as a difference; one that does not is reported as no
detectable difference, which is not the same as evidence that no effect exists.

\paragraph{Correctness of the Move Generator.}
All results rest on the assumption that the engine generates and applies moves
correctly. This is verified with \emph{perft}~\cite{cpw_perft}, the standard
correctness test for
chess engines: for a set of reference positions, the number of leaf nodes of the
full game tree is counted up to a fixed depth and compared against published
values. Any error in move generation, in make--unmake, or in the handling of
castling, en-passant and promotion changes these counts and is thus detected. The
engine reproduces the reference values for all tested positions and depths.

\subsection{Playing Strength (Engine versus Engine)}
\label{subsec:engine_eval}

As an external sanity check on the implementation, the engine is evaluated in
head-to-head matches against a fixed reference opponent. This experiment does not
place the engine on an absolute rating scale, it only relates it to one specific
opponent configuration. The matches are played through the CuteChess framework, with a
development build of Stockfish as the reference opponent. To obtain a comparison
in the target strength range, Stockfish is restricted through its built-in
strength limitation (\texttt{UCI\_LimitStrength} with a nominal target of
2000\,Elo), a single search thread, and a 32\,MB hash; Helix likewise runs
single-threaded with a 32\,MB hash and otherwise default settings. Both engines
play a blitz time control of five minutes per side with a 0.5\,second increment,
which is the range in which time management is most visible while still allowing
searches of useful depth.

To obtain a fair and diverse sample, the games are opened from a balanced opening
book (\texttt{8moves\_v3}), with each opening played once from either side so
that both engines receive identical starting positions as White and Black. In
total 400 games are played with alternating colors. Clearly decided games are
adjudicated to keep the match tractable: a game is scored as won once both
engines report an evaluation beyond $\pm 600$ centipawns for three consecutive
moves, and as drawn once the evaluation stays within $\pm 10$ centipawns for
eight consecutive moves from move 40 onwards. All games run on the Apple
M3 Pro machine described in \cref{subsec:common_setup}. Playing strength is
reported as the Elo difference derived from the match result by the testing
framework, together with the likelihood of superiority (LOS) and the draw ratio.

Two limitations apply to the reported interval. The games are played as
colour-swapped opening pairs, so the 400 games are not 400 independent trials;
the interval, computed by the framework under an independence assumption, is
therefore optimistic. And the LOS of $100\,\%$ is a rounded value, not a
probability of one.

Helix wins the match decisively, with an Elo difference of roughly $+205$ points
and a likelihood of superiority of $100\,\%$. The advantage holds with both
colors: Helix scores $138$--$40$--$22$ as White ($74.5\,\%$) and
$150$--$36$--$14$ as Black ($78.5\,\%$), so it remains clearly stronger even
without the first-move advantage. The moderate draw ratio of $9.0\,\%$ indicates
that most games are decided rather than drawn.

Two aspects of this result deserve interpretation. That the engine performs
slightly better as Black is not a property of the engine but of the opponent:
Stockfish's strength limitation introduces random inaccuracies, and a game in
which the weakened side moves first offers more opportunity for such an
inaccuracy to decide the game early. More importantly, the result contrasts with
the position-based measurements that follow. A margin of $205$\,Elo describes an
engine that plays soundly across a whole game, while
\cref{subsec:eval_outliers} shows that it fails badly in a small class of
positions. Both are true at once: the failures are concentrated in sparse
endgames, and games between engines of this strength difference are usually
decided long before such an endgame is reached. Game results and per-position
analysis therefore measure genuinely different things, which is the reason this
thesis relies on the latter.

It should be emphasized that this is a \emph{relative} result. The reference
engine was deliberately weakened, and Stockfish's \texttt{UCI\_LimitStrength}
provides only a nominal Elo target that does not map directly onto established
rating lists; it also weakens play partly by introducing random inaccuracies
rather than uniformly. The reported difference therefore characterizes Helix's
strength relative to this specific, equally configured opponent, not an absolute
rating.

\label{subsec:eval_nmp}

the largest measured effect on the engine. It was evaluated by running the full
time-budget sweep twice, once with pruning enabled and once disabled, on the
common suite. \cref{tab:nmp_depth_cpl} reports the reachable depth and the
centipawn loss for both configurations.

The clearest and most robust effect is on search depth: null-move pruning reaches
a greater depth at \emph{every} one of the nine budgets, and the gap widens with
more time, from $+0.23$\,ply at 10\,ms to a full ply ($8.21$ versus $7.12$) at one
second and $+1.35$\,ply at five seconds. This confirms that skipping provably
weak subtrees lets the engine invest the saved effort into depth.

This additional depth is not bought at the cost of node throughput. At the
one-second budget the engine reaches about $1.69$ million nodes per second with
pruning versus $1.61$ million without, so pruning is if anything slightly faster
per node as well. What does change is the transposition table hit rate, which
drops from $0.208$ to $0.158$ at one second: by pruning subtrees, the search
visits a larger number of \emph{distinct} positions and re-visits known entries
less often. The hit rate is therefore best read as a symptom of the search shape
rather than as a quality target in itself.

The effect on move quality does not follow the depth gain. The paired differences
scatter around zero: the pruning configuration is ahead at three of the nine
budgets and behind at six, the largest single values being $-3.1$\,cp at one
second and $+3.0$\,cp at 100\,ms. Crucially, the confidence interval contains
zero at \emph{every} budget, and the intervals are wide, spanning ten centipawns
or more, because the per-position differences vary far more than their mean. The
experiment therefore does not show that pruning leaves quality untouched; it
shows that any effect is small compared to the spread between positions and
cannot be resolved with this sample. The contrast to the depth column, where
every interval excludes zero by a wide margin, is what makes the comparison
informative.

Taken together, this is the central observation of this
experiment: null-move pruning reliably buys depth, while no corresponding gain in
move quality could be detected. One qualification matters for the interpretation.
Null-move pruning does not give the engine the same search plus one exact ply, it
changes which subtrees are explored at all; the comparison is therefore between
two search algorithms under equal time, not a clean measurement of what one
additional ply is worth. Isolating the latter would require the same algorithm
run at different fixed budgets. What the experiment does establish is that a
refinement which demonstrably deepens the search does not, on this suite,
demonstrably improve its decisions, and
\cref{subsec:eval_ttq,subsec:eval_outliers,subsec:eval_move_stability} examine
where that gap comes from. \cref{fig:quality_vs_depth,fig:search_nps} visualize
the quality and the node throughput across budgets.

\subsection{Transposition Table Size}
\label{subsec:eval_tt_size}

The transposition table trades memory for search efficiency: a larger table
retains more previously computed positions but consumes more memory. To determine
a sensible default, the table size was swept over $1, 4, 16, 64$, and $256$\,MB
at a fixed one-second budget, with null-move pruning enabled.
\cref{tab:tt_size} reports the reachable depth, the eviction rate (the share of
stores that overwrite an entry of a different position), and the hit rate.

4   & 8.01 & $-0.170$ [$-0.215$, $-0.128$] & 0.263 & 0.134 \\
16  & 8.14 & $-0.045$ [$-0.070$, $-0.023$] & 0.076 & 0.153 \\
64  & 8.18 & $-0.003$ [$-0.014$, $+0.005$] & 0.019 & 0.161 \\
256 & 8.18 & --- & 0.005 & 0.163 \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Effect of transposition table size at a one-second budget, over 309
positions and three repetitions. The third column gives the paired difference in
depth against the largest table with a 95\,\% bootstrap confidence interval over
positions.}
\label{tab:tt_size}
\end{table}

The cost of a small table is substantial and clearly resolved: at 1\,MB the search
loses $0.44$ ply against the largest table, with more than $60\,\%$ of all stores
evicting a foreign entry, so useful information is displaced before it can be
reused. The penalty then shrinks rapidly, to $0.17$ ply at 4\,MB and $0.05$ ply at
16\,MB. Both of these differences are still resolved by the data, so the curve
does not become flat as early as the raw means suggest; what changes is that the
effect becomes practically irrelevant, an order of magnitude below the ply gained
by null-move pruning. Only from 64\,MB onward is no difference to the largest
table detectable at all, even though the eviction rate keeps falling. For the
target time controls the search therefore stops being appreciably memory-bound
around the 32\,MB default used elsewhere in this work.

The shape of this curve follows from what the search actually needs. At a
one-second budget the engine visits roughly $1.5$ million nodes, and a 1\,MB table
holds about $32\,000$ entries, so it is overwritten many times over during a single
search: the $61\,\%$ eviction rate means most entries are displaced before they can
be reused, and the table degenerates from a memory into a cache of the most recent
positions. Every additional megabyte then buys fewer new hits than the previous
one, because entries are displaced in rough order of usefulness, and once the
table is large enough to survive one search the remaining gain comes only from
positions that recur rarely. That the curve flattens near the size actually needed
for one search, rather than at some arbitrary point, is what makes the result
transferable: an engine searching ten times as many nodes would need a
correspondingly larger table.

\subsection{Time-to-Quality and Depth-versus-Time}
\label{subsec:eval_ttq}

The per-budget measurements in \cref{tab:nmp_depth_cpl} also answer the
time-to-quality and depth-versus-time questions directly. Reachable depth grows
smoothly and predictably with the budget: a log-linear fit over all nine budgets
yields $0.63$ additional plies per doubling of time ($R^2 = 0.999$), and the
individual doublings stay within a narrow band around it, between $+0.63$ and
$+0.72$. Inverted, this corresponds to
an effective branching factor of $2^{1/0.63} \approx 3$, meaning each additional
ply costs about three times the work of the previous one. This is the exponential
relationship discussed in \cref{sec:preliminaries}, and it is the reason the
budget has to grow by a factor of roughly $500$ to buy the five and a half plies
separating the smallest budget from the largest.

Move quality, in contrast, improves far less smoothly. Over the whole range from
10\,ms to 5000\,ms, a factor of 500 in thinking time, the mean centipawn loss of
the pruning configuration falls only from about $62$ to $44$\,cp, and it does so
non-monotonically: at 100\,ms the curve even rises by $4$\,cp against the
preceding budget before continuing downwards. No single doubling of the budget produces a difference
whose confidence interval excludes zero; only comparisons spanning several steps
do, for instance from 500\,ms to 5000\,ms.

To avoid identifying a saturation point by eye, it is defined here explicitly.
Call a budget $t$ \emph{saturated} if no larger budget yields a paired
improvement whose $95\,\%$ confidence interval excludes zero, and let $t^\ast$ be
the smallest budget that is saturated and stays saturated:
\[
t \text{ saturated} \iff 0 \in \text{CI}_{95}\big(\text{CPL}(t') - \text{CPL}(t)\big)
\quad \text{for all } t' > t ,
\]
\[
t^\ast = \min\{\, t \;:\; \text{every } s \geq t \text{ is saturated} \,\} .
\]
The second condition is necessary because saturation alone is not monotone: at
the shortest budgets the per-position differences scatter so widely that nothing
is detectable there either, which would otherwise mark 50\,ms as a saturation
point. On this suite the criterion gives $t^\ast = 1000$\,ms. At 500\,ms a further
improvement towards 5000\,ms is still detectable, at one second it is not. The
median tells the same story more directly: it falls from $16$\,cp at 10\,ms over
$7$\,cp at 100\,ms to $2$\,cp at one second, and then stops, standing at $3$\,cp
at five seconds.

Two qualifications belong to this number. First, $t^\ast$ marks the point where
further improvement is no longer \emph{detectable} with this sample, not where it
provably ceases; the intervals are wide, so a small effect could remain hidden.
Second, mean and median diverge here in a way that matters: while the median
stops falling at one second, the mean still decreases from $52$ to $44$\,cp
between one and five seconds, because a minority of positions continues to
improve. \cref{subsec:eval_outliers} shows that this minority dominates the mean.
The defensible formulation is therefore that no further improvement of
\emph{typical} move quality is detectable beyond roughly one second on this
suite, while \emph{average} quality keeps improving through a small group of
difficult positions.

\subsection{Distribution of Move Quality}
\label{subsec:eval_outliers}

All centipawn losses reported so far are means over the suite, and that summary
turns out to hide the actual behavior of the engine. The distribution of the
per-position loss is strongly skewed: at a one-second budget the \emph{median}
loss is $2$\,cp while the \emph{mean} is $52.4$\,cp. In $49.0\,\%$ of all
positions the engine's move loses nothing at all against the reference: in
$43.8\,\%$ it is exactly the move Stockfish prefers, and in the remaining
$5.2\,\%$ a different move of equal reference value. In $66.3\,\%$ of the
positions the loss stays within $25$\,cp. At the other end, $6.9\,\%$ of the positions
(21 of 306) exceed $200$\,cp, and the fifteen worst positions alone account for
$46.3\,\%$ of the entire mean. The average is therefore not a description of the
typical case, it is a description of a small number of failures.

This also changes how the time-to-quality result of \cref{subsec:eval_ttq} should
be read. Measured by the median, additional time helps clearly and monotonically:
the median loss falls from $16$\,cp at 10\,ms over $7$\,cp at 100\,ms to $2$\,cp
at one second. The mean, dominated by the outliers, barely moves over the same
range. The engine does not gradually get better at every position, it solves more
and more positions outright while a hard residue stays unsolved regardless of the
budget.

The outliers share a recognizable profile. Positions with a loss above $200$\,cp
hold on average $13.8$ pieces, against $19.5$ for the well-played ones, and a
third of them are endgames with ten pieces or fewer. Three positions reach the
scoring cap of $1000$\,cp, meaning Stockfish sees a forced mate the engine misses
entirely.

Before examining individual cases, it is worth noting how the ten distribute.
Five of them are endgames with at most eight pieces beside the kings, two are

positions examined below, shown in \cref{fig:worst_positions}, are chosen as
representatives of these rather than as isolated curiosities: a plan that requires
more depth than the budget allows (position 22), a tactical resource that move
ordering never brings to the front (position 46), and a decision the evaluation
cannot make at any depth (position 25).

\paragraph{Position 22: a pawn breakthrough that is never seen.}
White has pawns on f2, f5, g4 and h5 against b7, f7, g7 and h6, with the kings on
c2 and c4. White's h-pawn is blocked by the black pawn on h6, so the position is
only winnable by opening that file. The reference move \texttt{f5f6} sacrifices a
pawn to deflect the g7 pawn, but its point emerges only two moves later: after
\texttt{f6 gxf6}, White must first play the quiet \texttt{f2f4} to bring up the
reserve pawn, so that the breakthrough \texttt{g4g5} can be met by
\texttt{f4xg5} \emph{whichever} pawn Black captures with. Both capture orders
transpose into the same position, in which the h-pawn is finally unblocked and
promotes in two moves, while Black's counterpart needs four and is caught by
White's king. Played immediately, without the preparation, \texttt{g4g5} fails to
\texttt{f6xg5}, after which White has no passed pawn at all.

The engine plays \texttt{g4g5} at every budget from 50\,ms upwards, and the
evaluation swings from $+507$ to $-658$\,cp: this is not a missed win but a lost
game. Three properties of the implementation combine here, and each alone would
suffice to miss the line. White is a pawn down for its entire length, and an
evaluation built on material and piece-square tables sees no compensation for
that. The decisive move \texttt{f2f4} is quiet, so the quiescence search, which
extends captures and en passant only (\cref{subsec:quiescence_impl}), never
looks at it; note that admitting promotions to the extension, the obvious
suspect, would not help here either, since every move of the plan up to the
promotion is quiet. And the promotion that justifies the whole operation lies thirteen
plies from the root, whereas the engine reaches depth 8 at a one-second budget.
This position is also the one case in the suite where the reported loss of $1000$
is a clamp rather than a mate: the raw difference is $1165$\,cp.

\paragraph{Position 46: a mating attack that is missed.}
The reference plays \texttt{g4g7}, moving the queen to an empty square directly in
front of the black king. Despite appearances this is not a sacrifice: no black
piece can capture on g7. The check leaves Black exactly one legal reply,
\texttt{Ke8}, after which the reference reports a forced mate. The engine plays
\texttt{c3c4} instead. This is not an evaluation problem at all, since a mate is a
purely tactical fact that any evaluation scores correctly once it is found; the
engine simply never searches far enough along this line. Move ordering is the
plausible reason, and in a direction that is easy to overlook. \texttt{Qg7+} is a
\emph{quiet} move, so MVV-LVA never applies to it and it is sorted behind every
capture in the list (\cref{subsec:move_ordering}). Only the killer and history
heuristics could lift it, and both require the move to have proved itself
elsewhere in the tree first, which is exactly what does not happen here. The
position illustrates that good move ordering is not merely a speed optimization:
at a fixed budget it decides which moves are examined at all.

\paragraph{Position 25: a draw turned into a loss.}
Black is nominally a pawn up (rook and three pawns against rook and two), yet the
reference evaluates the position as exactly balanced, because White's pawn on e6
and the active rook on the seventh rank compensate. Black's two h-pawns are
doubled, on h6 and h4, and the whole assessment hinges on which of them moves. The
reference plays \texttt{h4h3}, advancing the front pawn, which is three moves from
promotion and forces White to attend to it. The engine plays \texttt{h6h5},
advancing the rear pawn directly behind its own, where it is immediately blocked
and accomplishes nothing; the evaluation drops by roughly five pawns.

Both moves are pawn pushes on the same file and neither changes material by a
single point, so no material-based term can tell them apart. Distinguishing them
requires knowing that one pawn is a passer close to promotion and the other is
blocked by its own colleague, and the evaluation encodes neither passed pawns nor
pawn structure (\cref{subsec:eval_limitations}). This is the cleanest example in
the suite of a decision that additional search depth cannot repair, because the
engine would still be comparing the two resulting positions with an evaluation
that scores them almost identically.

\paragraph{A pattern across the outliers.}
These cases are not isolated. Comparing the 21 positions above $200$\,cp with the
203 played well ($\leq 25$\,cp) reveals a systematic difference in what the engine
chooses: in the outliers it plays a capture where the reference does not in
$19\,\%$ of cases, against $4\,\%$ in the well-played group. The reference, in
turn, moves its king in $24\,\%$ of the outliers but only $13\,\%$ elsewhere. The
engine thus errs in a specific direction. It takes material where a positional
move is required, and it overlooks king activity, the central resource in sparse
endgames. Both follow directly from an evaluation built on material and
piece-square tables, in combination with a quiescence search that resolves
captures and nothing else.

The pattern is consistent with the limitations of the evaluation function
discussed in \cref{subsec:eval_limitations}. Material and piece-square tables
describe a middlegame reasonably well, but they encode nothing about passed pawns,
opposition or king activity, the very concepts that decide sparse endgames. That
the affected positions are predominantly sparse, and that they are largely the
same ones which never stabilize in \cref{subsec:eval_move_stability}, supports
this reading, though the present experiments cannot separate it from search-side
explanations such as horizon effects. Under either reading, the concentration of
the error explains why an across-the-board depth gain such as the one from
null-move pruning need not show up in the mean: the positions where additional
depth would have to make the difference are precisely the few that dominate it.

\subsection{Effect of Principal Variation Search}
\label{subsec:eval_pvs}

Principal Variation Search is evaluated in two steps: first against a search that
does not scout at all, then across its own parameters. The first comparison
answers what the technique contributes, the second whether that contribution
depends on how it is tuned.

For the first, scouting was disabled entirely, so that every move is searched with
the full window $[\alpha, \beta]$, and the time-budget sweep was repeated with
null-move pruning active in both configurations. \cref{tab:pvs_onoff} reports the
result.

about $2.5\,\%$ \emph{more} nodes per second at one second
($1.69$ against $1.64$ million), because a null-window search produces cutoffs
more cheaply than a full-window one. Scouting therefore does not reduce the tree
at equal depth so much as it makes each node cheaper, which under a fixed clock
converts into additional depth. The transposition table hit rate rises alongside
it, from $0.124$ to $0.158$.

The comparison is limited to reachable depth. Since this experiment records no
reference scores, it does not establish whether the additional depth also
improves move quality, and \cref{subsec:eval_nmp} suggests that such a transfer
should not be assumed.

\subsubsection{Parameter Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:eval_pvs_sweep}

Given that scouting helps, the question is whether its two parameters matter: the
minimum scouting depth (\texttt{PvsMinDepth}) and the number of leading moves
searched with the full window (\texttt{PvsScoutAfterMove}). To find out whether
tuning them is worthwhile, a full grid of $6 \times 5 = 30$ configurations
(\texttt{PvsMinDepth} $1$ to $6$, \texttt{PvsScoutAfterMove} $1$ to $5$) was
evaluated on the full suite at a one-second budget with null-move pruning
enabled. \cref{tab:pvs_sweep} lists a representative selection.

The sweep is a negative result: across all 30 configurations the completed depth
stays between $8.09$ and $8.16$\,ply and the node count between $1.52$ and $1.55$
million, a spread of about $1.8\,\%$. Paired against the default configuration

disables scouting, by only $-0.064$\,ply, 95\,\% CI $[-0.096, -0.033]$. The
latter interval does exclude zero, so the sweep does detect a difference, but one
two orders of magnitude below the ply gained by null-move pruning. No setting
offers a practically relevant improvement over the default.

One limitation applies to this sweep: with 30 configurations compared against one
another, some differences of this size are expected by chance alone, and no
correction for multiple comparisons was applied. The result should be read as the
absence of a usable tuning gain, not as a precise ranking of the configurations.

Together with \cref{tab:pvs_onoff} this yields a two-part answer. Scouting itself
contributes measurably, but how aggressively it is applied does not: the decision
that matters is whether to scout at all, not where to start.

The reason is visible in the re-search column. The whole point of the scouting
parameters is to control the trade-off between the savings of the cheap
null-window scout and the cost of the occasional full-window re-search when the
scout fails high. In this engine that trade-off barely materializes: across the
whole grid the average number of re-searches per search ranges from about $3$ to
about $146$, against roughly $1.5$ million nodes visited, so even in the busiest
configuration fewer than one node in ten thousand is a re-search. The existing
move ordering (transposition-table move, MVV-LVA captures, killer moves, and the
history heuristic) is already good enough that the first move is almost always
the best, so the null-window scout almost always holds and there is little
re-search cost left for the parameters to tune away. As a side observation, the
transposition table hit rate correlates weakly with the number of re-searches
($0.158$ at $104$ re-searches versus $0.144$ at $3$), which again confirms that
the hit rate reflects the shape of the search rather than being an independent
quality target. Consequently, the default configuration
(\texttt{PvsMinDepth}\,$=2$, \texttt{PvsScoutAfterMove}\,$=1$) is retained, as no
setting in the sweep improves upon it.

\subsection{Search Tree Reuse}
\label{subsec:eval_tree_reuse}

The second refinement of this thesis is the reuse of previously explored search
trees through a persistent transposition table (\cref{subsec:tree_reuse}). Its
value cannot be measured on isolated positions, since it only materializes when
consecutive searches are related, so this experiment replays complete games
instead. The 30 games are taken from the match of \cref{subsec:engine_eval}, and
every position of every game is searched with a fixed budget of 500\,ms, once in
each of two modes: in \emph{keep} the table survives the whole game, which is the
engine's normal behavior, and in \emph{clear} it is wiped before every single
search. Both runs see identical positions under identical budgets, so the
difference between them is precisely the value of resuming the old tree.
\cref{tab:tree_reuse} reports the aggregate over all 2256 plies.

\begin{table}[ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Mode & Completed depth (ply) & TT hit rate & Nodes \\ \midrule
TT cleared each move & 7.23 & 0.136 & 758\,979 \\
TT kept (tree reuse) & 7.52 & 0.152 & 772\,368 \\ \bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Effect of search tree reuse over 30 complete games (2256 plies) at a
500\,ms budget per move.}
\label{tab:tree_reuse}
\end{table}

Keeping the table yields more depth at an unchanged time budget, together with a
higher hit rate. The 2256 plies are not independent observations, since they are
clustered within 30 games, so the effect is quantified per game: averaging the
paired per-ply differences within each game and bootstrapping over games gives
$+0.254$\,ply, 95\,\% CI $[+0.218, +0.293]$, and $+0.013$ in hit rate,
95\,\% CI $[+0.010, +0.018]$. The effect is consistent rather than driven by
individual games: all 30 games show a positive mean difference. Treating the
plies as independent would have produced a visibly narrower interval and thereby
overstated the precision.

A paired comparison per ply shows how the average comes about: in $69\,\%$ of all
plies both modes reach exactly the same depth, in $28\,\%$ the persistent table
reaches deeper, and only in the remaining few does the cleared table win. Tree
reuse thus does not speed up every single search, it provides a head start in
those positions where the new root actually lies inside the previously explored
tree.

That reading is supported by the development over the course of a game, shown in
\cref{fig:tree_reuse}. The gain grows from $+0.16$\,ply in the opening
(plies 0--15) over $+0.22$ in the middlegame to $+0.39$ in the endgame
(ply 41 onwards), where the hit rate also climbs to $0.207$ versus $0.178$. This
is consistent with a larger share of the stored tree remaining reachable as
material decreases, since positions with fewer pieces admit more transpositions.
The experiment measures the hit rate, however, not the number of transpositions
itself, so this remains an interpretation of the observed pattern rather than a
measured mechanism.

Compared to null-move pruning, the effect is smaller in absolute terms ($+0.25$
versus a full ply), but it is obtained differently. Unlike null-move pruning,
persistence introduces no additional forward-pruning heuristic; it reuses bounds
and moves that a previous search already computed exactly. Whether this also
changes move quality was not measured here, since the experiment records depth
and table statistics but does not score the played moves against a reference.

\subsection{Move Stability}
\label{subsec:eval_move_stability}

The final question concerns move stability: at which depth does the engine's
chosen move settle, so that deeper search no longer changes the root decision?
This is measured with a single iterative-deepening search per position. Because
iterative deepening already produces a best move at every completed depth, one
search yields the full sequence of root moves without repeated re-searching. Each
position is searched under a fixed budget of 20 million nodes with the
transposition table cleared beforehand, so that the depth sequence reflects one
self-contained search. A node budget is used instead of a fixed target depth
because it bounds the cost per position and, being independent of wall-clock time,
is unaffected by other load on the machine.

For each position the \emph{stabilization depth} is defined as the smallest depth
from which the root move matches the move of the deepest completed iteration and
does not change again \emph{within the searched range}. Positions whose move still
changes at the deepest searched depth are counted as not yet settled within the
budget. Of the 309 positions, 306 produced a usable move sequence, reaching a mean
depth of $10.1$ plies. \cref{fig:move_stability} shows the cumulative distribution
and \cref{tab:move_stability} lists selected thresholds.

This definition can only observe what happens inside the budget, which has to be
kept in mind when reading the numbers. A move that survives until the last
completed iteration might still change one ply deeper, and the evidence for a late
stabilization is necessarily thinner than for an early one: a position that
settles at depth 3 and is searched to depth 11 confirms that move eight more
times, whereas one that settles at depth 10 out of 11 confirms it once. The
measurement quantifies this directly. On average a stabilized move is confirmed by
$4.3$ further iterations (median 3), but for $22.2\,\%$ of the positions the move
changed in the very last iteration, which is why those are reported as unsettled
rather than as stabilized at the final depth. Restricting the analysis to the 178
positions whose move is confirmed by at least three further iterations shifts the
median stabilization depth down to 3 plies, with $87\,\%$ settled by depth 6.
That subset is itself selective, since it excludes late stabilizers by
construction, so the two views complement rather than replace one another. Note
that the censoring can bias the reported depths in either direction: a move that
looks settled may still change beyond the horizon, in which case the true
stabilization depth is larger, while restricting the analysis to well-confirmed
cases discards exactly the late stabilizers and thus reports smaller depths. All
figures below should therefore be read as \emph{observed} stabilization within
the searched range, not as the depth ultimately required.

The root move settles early for most positions: the median observed stabilization
depth is 6 plies, and $23.9\,\%$ of positions never change their move within the
searched range at all. By depth 8, roughly the depth the engine reaches at a
one-second budget (\cref{tab:nmp_depth_cpl}), three quarters of all positions have
stopped changing their move for the remainder of the search. Within the tested
range, additional search beyond this point keeps increasing depth but only rarely
changes the decision.

At the same time, the effect is not uniform. Roughly one position in five
($22.2\,\%$) still changes its move at the deepest depth searched and is thus not
observed to stabilize at all. These are the positions whose root decision remains
depth-sensitive within the tested range, and they are the only ones for which the
extra ply bought by null-move pruning (\cref{subsec:eval_nmp}) can change anything.
Since they make up only a fifth of the suite, an across-the-board gain in depth is
diluted accordingly, which is consistent with the depth gain of null-move pruning
leaving the average centipawn loss unchanged. Decision stability is therefore
concentrated: the majority of decisions are made early, while a minority remains
sensitive to further search.

\subsubsection{Stability in Evaluation Rather Than in the Move}
\label{subsubsec:eval_eps_stability}

Judging stability by the identity of the move has a blind spot: a switch between
two moves of equal value counts as instability, while converging on a
consistently poor move counts as success. To separate these cases, every move of
every iteration was additionally scored against the reference engine, and a
position is called \emph{$\varepsilon$-stable} from the smallest depth at which
its centipawn loss stays below $\varepsilon$ for all deeper iterations.
\cref{tab:eps_stability} contrasts the two criteria and \cref{fig:eps_stability}
shows the corresponding curves.

The two criteria disagree in both directions, and the curves cross at around
depth 6. Below that, judging by evaluation reports \emph{more} stability: at
depth 3 already $39.7\,\%$ of the positions are within 20\,cp, against $31.8\,\%$
whose move has settled. The engine reaches a decision of adequate quality earlier
than it settles on one particular move, since part of the apparent instability is
merely alternation between moves of comparable value.

Above depth 6 the relation reverses, and this is the more consequential half. By
move identity, $88.9\,\%$ of the positions have settled by depth 10; measured
against the reference evaluation, only $63.3\,\%$ are within 20\,cp, and
$33.1\,\%$ never reach that margin at any depth. These are not positions in which
the move keeps changing: their move does settle, but it settles on something
poor, with a mean loss of $120$\,cp at the deepest iteration. Fourteen of the 21
worst positions from \cref{subsec:eval_outliers} are among them.

Both readings therefore support the same conclusion from opposite sides. Where
the engine is able to find a good move, it finds it early, earlier than the
move-identity criterion suggests. Where it is not, the search converges
confidently on the wrong answer. Stability of the move is thus a necessary but
not a sufficient indicator of a settled decision.

It is worth asking whether these positions would simply need more depth. Within
the tested range, the data argue against it. Both groups are searched to the same
average depth of $10.1$ plies under the shared node budget, so the difference is
not one of search effort. What differs is how much that effort achieves:
comparing the first half of each search to the second, and excluding the final
iteration that defines the grouping, the loss falls by $36.5$\,cp on average for
the positions that do converge, but only by $5.4$\,cp for those that do not. The
same picture emerges when comparing shallow to intermediate depths, where the
converging positions gain $41.5$\,cp and the others $21.9$\,cp. Additional depth
is therefore not without effect on the difficult positions, but it is roughly
half to one seventh as effective, and it starts from a worse level.

What this establishes is that the two groups differ in how well they convert
depth into quality, not why. The observation is consistent with the evaluation
being the binding constraint: the affected positions are materially sparse
(\cref{subsec:eval_outliers}), and material with piece-square tables encodes
nothing about passed pawns, opposition or king activity, which is exactly what
decides such positions. Other explanations cannot be excluded by this experiment,
however, among them horizon effects in long forcing lines, the absence of search
extensions, or degraded move ordering in sparse positions. Establishing the cause
would require an intervention rather than an observation: adding the missing
evaluation terms and re-running the affected subset under an identical node
budget. If the group improved materially, the argument would be direct. That
experiment is left to future work; what can be stated here is that within the
depths this engine reaches, the difficult positions do not respond to depth the
way the rest of the suite does.
