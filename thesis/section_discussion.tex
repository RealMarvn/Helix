\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

The experiments of \cref{sec:evaluation} allow the central question of this
thesis to be answered, and the answer is more nuanced than the initial
formulation suggested. This section relates the individual results to that
question, draws the overall conclusions, and outlines where the engine would have
to be extended for the remaining gap to close.

\subsection{Depth and Decision Quality Are Not Proportional}
\label{subsec:discussion_depth_vs_quality}

The clearest finding of this work is that additional search depth does not
translate into proportionally better decisions. Null-move pruning
(\cref{subsec:eval_nmp}) reaches a greater depth at every single one of the nine
time budgets, at one second a full ply, with confidence intervals that exclude
zero throughout; the corresponding intervals for centipawn loss contain zero at
every budget. Search tree reuse (\cref{subsec:eval_tree_reuse}) adds another
$0.25$ ply, 95\,\% CI $[+0.22, +0.29]$, without changing any search parameter.
Both refinements demonstrably deepen the search, and for neither could a
corresponding gain in move quality be detected on this suite.

The move stability measurements (\cref{subsec:eval_move_stability}) offer an
explanation. By depth 8, the depth the engine reaches at a one-second budget,
three quarters of all positions have stopped changing their move within the
searched range, and about a quarter never change it from the very first
iteration. Deeper search can only change the decision in the remaining fifth, so
an across-the-board gain in depth is diluted accordingly. Applying the criterion
of \cref{subsec:eval_ttq}, the smallest budget beyond which no further
improvement is detectable is one second for this engine and suite.

That number should be read for what it is. It describes typical positions, where
the median loss stops falling, while the mean keeps decreasing because a minority
of difficult positions continues to improve. And it marks the point where a
difference is no longer \emph{resolved} by this sample, not where improvement
provably ends: the intervals are wide enough that a small effect could remain
hidden.

\subsection{Where the Search Is Actually Limited}
\label{subsec:discussion_limits}

The distribution of move quality (\cref{subsec:eval_outliers}) shows where the
remaining errors come from. The engine plays exactly Stockfish's move in
$49\,\%$ of the positions and stays within $25$\,cp in two thirds of them, with a
median loss of $2$\,cp. The mean of $52$\,cp is produced almost entirely by a
small residue: fifteen positions account for nearly half of it.

These positions share a profile. They hold fewer pieces than average, a third of
them are endgames, and three contain a forced mate the engine does not find.
These are the positions in which material and piece-square tables carry little
useful information, since passed pawns, opposition and king activity are not
represented in the evaluation (\cref{subsec:eval_limitations}). The observation
is therefore consistent with evaluation quality being the binding constraint
there, although the experiments presented here cannot separate it from other
candidates such as horizon effects or missing search extensions.

Judging stability by evaluation instead of by the identity of the move makes the
consequence explicit (\cref{subsubsec:eval_eps_stability}). While the move
settles in nearly every position, a third of them never come within 20\,cp of the
reference at any depth, ending at an average loss of $120$\,cp. The search does
not remain undecided in these cases, it converges, and it converges on the wrong
move. A stable decision is therefore not by itself a good one, which is worth
keeping in mind wherever move stability is used as a stopping criterion in
time management.

What can be shown directly is that these positions do not respond to depth the way
the rest of the suite does. They are searched just as deeply, to the same average
of $10.1$ plies, but convert that depth into far less: over the course of a search
their loss improves by $5.4$\,cp where the converging positions gain $36.5$\,cp.
Depth is not useless there, it is merely a much weaker lever against a gap of more
than $100$\,cp. This rules out insufficient search effort as the explanation, but
it does not by itself identify the evaluation as the cause; it makes it the most
plausible candidate, given which positions are affected. Deciding the question
requires an intervention on the evaluation itself, which is the single most
valuable experiment this thesis leaves open.

The Principal Variation Search results (\cref{subsec:eval_pvs}) fit the same
picture from a different angle. Scouting itself is worth having: it gains
$0.23$\,ply at a one-second budget, with intervals excluding zero at every budget.
Its parameters, however, are irrelevant, since the node count varies by less than
two percent across all 30 configurations. The reason is that re-searches are rare
to begin with, about 104 over 1.5 million nodes even under the most aggressive
setting. The move ordering, built from transposition table moves, MVV-LVA, killer
moves and the history heuristic, is already good enough that the first move
examined is almost always the best one, so the trade-off the parameters are meant
to control barely arises. This is consistent with the long-standing observation
that the effectiveness of Alpha-Beta pruning is governed by move ordering rather
than by the pruning scheme itself~\cite{knuth1975alphabeta, marsland1986review}.

Notably, scouting buys its depth not by shrinking the tree but by making each node
cheaper: with scouting the engine visits more nodes per second, not fewer. All
three refinements studied here therefore increase reachable depth by different
means, and none of them was shown to improve move quality.

\subsection{Implications for Time-Managed Search}
\label{subsec:discussion_time}

For iterative deepening under time constraints~\cite{korf1985iterative} these
results are encouraging. The anytime property is not merely a safety net: since
most positions settle early, an interrupted search usually returns the same move
a deeper one would have confirmed. Stopping between iterations therefore costs
far less quality than the depth difference alone would suggest.

The measurements also show that the engine stops being appreciably memory-bound
around the default hash size (\cref{subsec:eval_tt_size}). A small table costs
real depth, but the penalty falls below a twentieth of a ply by 16\,MB and is no
longer detectable at 64\,MB. Set against the tree-reuse result, this suggests that
keeping the table alive across moves is worth more than enlarging it: retaining
what was already computed gains $0.25$ ply, whereas quadrupling the table from
64\,MB upwards gains nothing measurable~\cite{warnock1988search}.

Against a fixed reference opponent, the engine wins its match by roughly $205$
Elo points with both colors (\cref{subsec:engine_eval}). This is a check that the
implementation performs sensibly as a whole, not a rating: the figure describes
the relation to one weakened Stockfish configuration and would change with any
other opponent.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:discussion_limitations}

Several limitations qualify these conclusions. Move quality is measured against a
strong reference engine rather than against ground truth, so agreement with
Stockfish is treated as a proxy for correctness; positions in which a different
move is equally good are penalised by the agreement metric, which is why
centipawn loss is used as the primary measure.

The suite consists of 309 predefined positions with three repetitions each. It is
large enough for the aggregate effects reported here, but the skewed distribution
of the loss means that individual means are sensitive to a small number of
positions, and differences of a few centipawns should not be over-interpreted.

The stability results are bounded by the observation horizon. A move counts as
stabilized when it stops changing within a 20-million-node search, which cannot
say what a deeper search would do; roughly a fifth of the positions still change
their move at the deepest iteration reached. The comparison between converging
and non-converging positions inherits this limitation and is additionally
descriptive by construction, since the groups are defined by the very quality
behavior whose trajectories are then compared. Defining the groups in advance, by
piece count or game phase, would make that comparison independent.

The opponent in the match was deliberately weakened through Stockfish's built-in
strength limitation. That mechanism provides only a nominal Elo target and works
partly by introducing random inaccuracies, so the reported difference describes
the relation to this specific configuration and not an absolute rating.

Finally, the engine uses a mailbox board representation and a handcrafted
evaluation, both chosen for clarity rather than for strength. Modern engines rely
on bitboards~\cite{cpw_bitboards} and learned evaluation
functions~\cite{silver2018alphazero}, and the conclusions about absolute playing
strength do not transfer to those designs.

\subsection{Future Work}
\label{subsec:discussion_future}

The results point to one clear priority, and it takes the form of a testable
hypothesis rather than a conclusion. The static evaluation is the most plausible
candidate for the residual error and therefore the most valuable next
intervention: adding passed-pawn, king-activity and pawn-structure terms together
with dedicated endgame knowledge, and re-running the difficult subset under an
identical node budget. If that group improves materially while the rest of the
suite stays unchanged, the interpretation offered here would become direct
evidence; if it does not, the cause lies elsewhere in the search, which would be
an equally informative outcome. Either way this is the experiment that would turn
the present interpretation into a result.

A second direction concerns the measurement itself. A single mean is a poor
summary of a distribution this skewed, and the answer is not to remove the
extreme cases, which are the engine's real failures and among its most
informative observations, but to report several estimands side by side: the mean
as expected loss, the median as typical loss, upper percentiles, the share of
positions within a given margin, and the rate of catastrophic errors. Each
answers a different question, and stratifying them by position type would show
which refinement helps which class of position instead of averaging the two
regimes into one number.

The stability analysis could be extended in the same spirit. Judging convergence
by evaluation rather than by the identity of the move already proved more
informative here (\cref{subsubsec:eval_eps_stability}); applying the same
criterion to the time-to-quality measurements, and reporting from which
\emph{budget} rather than from which depth the loss stays within a margin, would
describe the practical behavior of the engine under a clock more directly than
the depth-based view can.

Finally, the search refinements evaluated here are only the beginning of what a
classical engine typically employs. Late move reductions, aspiration windows and
static exchange evaluation all target the same goal of spending effort where it
changes the decision, and the move ordering measured here is strong enough that
they would likely pay off.

\subsection{Summary}

This thesis implemented a complete chess engine and used it to examine how
invested search time translates into decision quality on a fixed position suite.
The central finding is that this translation is not uniform. Additional
computation reliably increases search depth, but the value of that depth differs
sharply between positions, and averaging over the suite hides two regimes rather
than describing one.

The majority of positions reaches a low centipawn loss early and stops changing
its move at modest depth; the median loss is $2$\,cp, and further time changes
little. A minority behaves in the opposite way: it accounts for nearly half of the
mean loss, keeps improving with additional time, and responds to depth far more
weakly than the rest, gaining $5.4$\,cp over a search where the others gain
$36.5$. These positions are predominantly sparse endgames.

All three refinements studied deliver what they promise in terms of search. At a
one-second budget null-move pruning gains a full ply, Principal Variation Search
$0.23$ ply and search tree reuse $0.25$ ply, each with $95\,\%$ intervals
excluding zero, and each by a different mechanism: pruning subtrees, making
individual nodes cheaper, and retaining what was already computed. For none of
them could a corresponding improvement in move quality be detected, which follows
from the heterogeneity above: a gain in depth applies to all positions, but only a
fifth of them are still in a state where depth changes the decision.

Under an explicit criterion, no further improvement of typical move quality is
detectable beyond roughly one second per move on this suite. This is an
operational statement rather than a claim that improvement ends there. What the
evidence supports, without establishing it conclusively, is that beyond this
point the limit lies less in how far the engine looks ahead than in what its
evaluation is able to distinguish when it arrives.
